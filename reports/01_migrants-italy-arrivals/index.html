Migrants in Italy: a historical portrait

Are migration flows to Italy historically exceptional in 2024? The answer depends on which phenomenon you measure — and that distinction is precisely what is missing from current political debate.

For total immigrations — all registered entries of persons transferring their residence to Italy — 2024 is not a record. With 451,583 arrivals, Italy is below the historical peak of the series, which occurred in 2008 with 534,712 persons (906 immigrants per 100,000 residents). 2024 is the highest value since 2012, but is 15% below the all-time high. The 2003 spike (440,301 arrivals) is attributable to the Bossi-Fini regularisation amnesty. The long-run curve describes a country that has already gone through more intense inflow phases, without this generating the same emotional charge in the public agenda.

In the European comparison, Italy in 2024 ranks 29th out of 32 countries by immigration rate — with 766 arrivals per 100,000 residents against a European average exceeding 1,400. Countries such as Spain (2,650/100k), Ireland (2,386/100k), Austria (1,406/100k) and Germany (1,292/100k) record far higher rates. In relative terms, Italy is not a high-migration-pressure country within the European picture.

The picture changes radically for asylum applications. This series, available from 1985, shows an all-time high in 2024 with 158,605 applications. The dynamic is structurally different from that of total immigrations: minimal levels until 2010 (except the Albanian peak in 1991 with 24,490 applications), a first rise with the Mediterranean crisis of 2011–2017 (peak of 128,855 in 2017), and a new record in 2023–2024. Asylum applications and total immigrations are two phenomena with independent dynamics — they are not a single curve.

The distinction is editorially essential: those invoking an unprecedented emergency are right when looking at asylum applications — genuinely at a historical high — and wrong when looking at total immigrations, which exceeded current levels as far back as 2007–2008. Collapsing the two phenomena into a single narrative is not merely imprecise: it obscures the causes, the populations involved, and the appropriate policy levers for each.
